What company filings show: revenue growth, margins & cash generation

A transparent, cross-company illustration using historical SEC annual 10-K facts. All charts and calculations are generated from the public QuantForge snapshot—without stock-price feeds, analyst forecasts, or personal portfolio data.

Historical disclosures only · Not investment advice

Research question

How do reported revenue growth, operating profitability, and cash generation vary across four public companies? This case study demonstrates reproducible financial measurement and provenance—not which investment to buy.

Evidence-based observations

  1. AMD has the highest latest observed year-over-year revenue change within this four-company illustration (+34.3%). This is not a stock-return ranking.
  2. 4 of 4 issuers have a positive free-cash-flow-to-revenue proxy in their latest included annual period. This is not a standardized definition of free cash flow or a valuation.
  3. 0 of 12 latest-period comparison cells are unavailable. Missing values are withheld rather than filled with zero.

Methods, decisions and limitations

Computation and reproducibility

Revenue growth = (latest revenue / previous eligible annual revenue − 1) × 100. Operating margin = operating income / annual revenue × 100. The cash-flow proxy = operating cash flow − payments to acquire property, plant and equipment; its revenue margin uses the same annual period.

The sample comprises MSFT, AMD, ADBE, and AMAT selected in advance for illustration. The values come only from the pinned web/site/sec_snapshot.json dataset. No network call is made by the build script.

What this analysis cannot establish

SEC annual facts are not adjusted stock-price histories, market capitalizations, point-in-time backtests, enterprise valuations, or predictions. Disclosure timing, restatements, accounting choices, sector economics and different fiscal dates limit cross-company comparability.

Company Facts may contain facts filed after the financial year they describe. Where present, the issuer extraction cutoff describes the published snapshot, not historical information availability; for some original issuers that cutoff is unrecorded. This analysis is unsuitable as an unbiased historical investment signal.
